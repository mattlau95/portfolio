Case study

pocalab

Making a photocard by hand meant re-cropping the same image in Canva, printing the fronts and backs on separate sheets, and gluing them together. Pocalab does the layout and gives you one PDF that prints both sides in register.

Role: Design + Engineering (solo) · June–September 2026 · Shipped

  • React 19
  • TypeScript
  • Vite
  • pdf-lib
  • Vercel

Live demo Repo

Overview

I make photocards. They're a K-pop collecting thing, and the point is that they match: 55 × 85 mm, double-sided, same stock every time, so a card you made sits in a binder next to an official one without announcing itself. I made them in a Canva template with the bleed and cut lines drawn in by hand. Pocalab is that template turned into a tool.

The problem

The manual version broke in two places. Every card meant re-cropping the same image to the same frame, checking it against guides I'd drawn myself, and doing it again for the back. Then, because a home printer won't hold alignment across a duplex pass, I printed fronts and backs on separate sheets and glued them together: 15 to 20 minutes of adhesive, careful alignment, and bubbles, on every sheet.

The second part is what made it worth building. Gluing wasn't a step I wanted to speed up. It only existed because the printing was wrong, and if the printing were right it would disappear.

Role & constraints

Solo build, and the whole thing is a static React app with no backend, no accounts, and no network calls. Your images never leave the browser, which for photos of your own face is the correct default and also means there's nothing to run.

The constraints came from the paper, not the software. A card is 59 × 89 mm with bleed and 55 × 85 mm trimmed, and those numbers have to survive three unit systems that never meet: the crop canvas works in pixels at 300 DPI, layout works in millimetres, and PDF works in points at 72 to the inch. One pixel at 300 DPI is 0.085 mm. That sounds like nothing until four files have each decided independently what a millimetre is.

Approach

Design

The crop editor is the product, so it shows the physical card rather than a rectangle. Four guide systems overlay the frame: bleed, trim, safe area, and thirds, with a key underneath that says in plain words what each one means. Most people cropping a photocard have never had to think about why the image has to run past the cut line, and the editor is where they find out.

Zooming below 100% is allowed on purpose. The image can sit inside the frame with a background colour around it instead of being forced to fill, which is how a lot of the cards I actually want to print are laid out. A low-resolution warning appears but never blocks the export. Printing something slightly soft is a choice the person makes, not an error.

Three colour tokens exist purely because of contrast maths, and the reason is written into the CSS next to them. The brand pink fails AA as a fill behind white text at 3.49:1, so filled controls use a darker pink, and the pink used as text needs different values on each theme to clear 4.5:1. The ratios are in the file so the next person to touch them knows why they can't just use the brand colour.

Engineering

Duplex registration comes down to one line. The back page mirrors each card across the sheet's vertical centre, back.x = sheetWidth − front.x − cardWidth, so that when the paper flips on its long edge every back lands behind its own front. Get it wrong and every card on the sheet is offset by the same amount, which looks like a printer problem and isn't. It's asserted directly in the tests, and the same mirrored geometry drives the crop marks on the back page.

The layout function also decides whether a sheet is physically possible, by checking that the margins are at least as wide as the bleed. That's why the photo-paper presets are single-column: two 85 mm cards side by side need 176 mm, and a 4 × 6 sheet is 101.6 mm. The tightest preset that does fit clears its bleed by 0.75 mm, and the UI says so.

Letter and photo paper started as two separate PDF pipelines, which is the sort of thing that's fine until the second one hard-codes its own copy of the card size. Merging them into one builder was the largest change in the project. I wrote the safety net first: tests that decode each page's PDF content stream, normalise the image names, round to a thousandth of a point, and snapshot the result. After the merge, all six paper presets produced byte-identical output. That's the evidence the refactor didn't move a single mark on the page.

Key decisions & tradeoffs

Rounding, not flooring, millimetres to pixels
The conversion floored, so a 59 mm bleed came out 696 px while every document said 697. I found it because a skipped test had been written against the documentation instead of the behaviour. The first fix corrected the docs down to 696. Twenty minutes later I reversed it: 697 is the closest whole pixel to the physical size, so the code should round and the documents were right all along. Crops exported before the change are a pixel narrower, so the app still recognises the old width when you re-upload one.
Borderless printing off, after telling people to turn it on

The app used to recommend borderless mode for photo paper. A session with the actual printer found that borderless silently scales the page up 2 to 3% to bleed off the edges, and that this was the cause of both the wrong card size and the front-to-back misalignment I'd been seeing. The presets leave real margins, so nothing needs to reach the paper edge. The advice in the app is now the opposite of what it was, and the original wrong answer is still in the history.

The whole project in one IndexedDB record
Persistence started in localStorage and died at three or four cards, because each card holds two full-resolution PNGs, about 1.24 MB, against a 5 MB quota. It failed quietly: one toast, and then every later edit was simply not saved. The fix keeps the entire project as a single IndexedDB entry through a small hand-written wrapper, with no new dependency and no change to the data model, so it stayed a storage change and nothing else.

Outcome

Live at pocalab.app since June 13, 2026. For my own printing, a nine-card sheet went from about 45 minutes to about 12, and the gluing step is gone. That's my own measurement of my own workflow, so read it as what it is. In-person testing with friends changed the product at least once: someone assumed the tool made a single card, which is why the first screen now says you're building a deck.

There's a feedback form after every download.

Reflection

The documentation and the code disagreed about one pixel for three months, and a test had quietly encoded the documentation rather than the behaviour, so nothing failed. It was the kind of error that can only surface when something forces the two to meet.

The feature I was most excited about at the start, a two-hole calibration trick for printers that can't hold registration on their own, still isn't built. It turned out most of what it was meant to fix was actually the borderless setting, which took a session with the printer to find. The unbuilt clever feature and the setting that was the real answer are both still in the backlog notes.
